1. WHY THIS MATTERS
  - Module 1 introduced vectors, matrices, and the operations you
    can do with them, mostly as mathematical objects. This lecture
    turns them around and uses them "in a data science way" to
    solve an actual problem.
  - The shape of the problem: you have a table of what people
    bought, you have what each person paid, and you want the price
    of each item. Nobody handed you a price list. The prices are
    hidden inside the receipts, and linear algebra is how you pull
    them back out.
  - Why this is the right first problem (added explanation): it is
    the smallest honest case of the thing data science does all
    day. Known inputs, known outputs, unknown weights in between.
    A linear regression fit is this exact setup with more rows
    and noisier numbers.

2. SETTING UP THE PROBLEM AS A MATRIX
  - The lecture builds a three by three matrix M where each column
    is a product and each row is a shopper. The entry in a row and
    column is how many of that product that shopper bought. The
    matrix stores purchasing behavior.
  - Each shopper's total from the register goes into a vector, the
    cost vector c, of length three. One total per shopper, in the
    same row order as the matrix.
  - The unknowns are the item prices, collected in a price vector
    p, also length three, one entry per product in the same column
    order as the matrix. You do not know these values yet.
  - The relationship that ties the three together: when M acts on
    p, the result must be c. Written compactly, M p = c. Two of the
    three pieces are known (M and c) and one is unknown (p).
  - For example, a coffee shop sells espresso, lattes, and muffins.
    Three regulars come through. Dana buys 2 espressos, 1 latte,
    1 muffin and pays 15. Eli buys 1 espresso, 3 lattes, 2 muffins
    and pays 26. Faye buys 1 espresso, 2 lattes, 2 muffins and pays
    21. As a matrix and two vectors:

      Q = [[2, 1, 1],        c = [15, 26, 21]       p = [p1, p2, p3]
           [1, 3, 2],
           [1, 2, 2]]

    Rows are Dana, Eli, Faye. Columns are espresso, latte, muffin.
    The unknowns p1, p2, p3 are the three prices in that order.
  - KEY TERM: cost vector: the vector of observed totals, one per
    row of the matrix. The known right-hand side.
  - KEY TERM: price vector: the vector of unknowns you are solving
    for, one per column of the matrix.
  - Reading the matrix (added explanation): pick a row, read across,
    and you have one person's shopping list. Pick a column, read
    down, and you have how popular one product was. The matrix is
    just the data table you would keep anyway. The linear algebra
    view adds one thing: the table is also a machine that turns a
    price list into a set of receipts.

3. TURNING THE MATRIX INTO EQUATIONS
  - M p = c is compact, but to work with it you write out what the
    matrix product actually does. Each row of M takes a dot product
    with p, and that dot product must equal the matching entry of
    c. One row, one dot product, one equation.
  - The lecture's point: when you write the problem this way, you
    have defined a system of linear equations, one per shopper.
    Each row of the matrix is one equation in the system.
  - For the coffee shop, the three dot products spell out as:

      2p1 + 1p2 + 1p3 = 15     (Dana's row)
      1p1 + 3p2 + 2p3 = 26     (Eli's row)
      1p1 + 2p2 + 2p3 = 21     (Faye's row)

  - Three equations, three unknowns (p1, p2, p3). The task is to
    find values for the unknowns that make all three equations
    true at the same time.
  - KEY TERM: system of linear equations: a set of equations that
    share the same unknowns, where each unknown appears multiplied
    by a plain number and the terms are added. No squares, no
    products of unknowns. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: linear: each unknown is only ever scaled and added,
    never squared, multiplied by another unknown, or fed through a
    function like a square root. That is what keeps the geometry
    flat (lines, planes) and the algebra solvable by substitution.
    (added definition)
  - Why the count matters (added explanation): each equation is one
    fact about the prices. With three unknowns you need three
    independent facts to pin them all down. Lecture 3 goes into
    what happens when you have fewer, or when a "fact" is really a
    repeat of another one.

4. THE STRATEGY: ONE UNKNOWN AT A TIME
  - The lecture's plan is substitution. Take one unknown, use one
    equation to write it as a formula in terms of the remaining
    unknowns, then push that formula into every other equation so
    the unknown disappears from them. Repeat with the next unknown
    on the equations that are left. Each round the system shrinks
    by one equation and one unknown.
  - Once you reach an equation with a single unknown, solve it as a
    number. Then walk back up: substitute that number into the
    formula for the previous unknown, get another number, and so
    on until every unknown is a number.
  - The two directions have a feel to them (added explanation).
    Going down, you are trading equations for formulas and making
    the problem smaller. Coming back up, you are cashing the
    formulas in for numbers. Lecture 2 names the whole procedure.
  - The mechanics of "write p1 as a formula": subtract the other
    terms from both sides, then divide both sides by whatever
    number is multiplying p1. The same two moves work for every
    step.

5. WORKING THE EXAMPLE FORWARD
  - Start with Dana's equation and isolate p1. Subtract p2 and p3
    from both sides, then divide by 2:

      p1 = 15/2 - (1/2)p2 - (1/2)p3

  - Substitute that expression for p1 into Eli's and Faye's
    equations. Eli's becomes 15/2 - (1/2)p2 - (1/2)p3 + 3p2 + 2p3
    = 26. Faye's becomes 15/2 - (1/2)p2 - (1/2)p3 + 2p2 + 2p3 = 21.
  - Combine like terms in each. Eli's p2 terms (3 and -1/2) add to
    5/2, his p3 terms (2 and -1/2) add to 3/2, and moving the 15/2
    across leaves 37/2 on the right. Multiply through by 2 to clear
    the halves. Faye's works the same way and lands on 3p2 + 3p3 =
    27, which divides by 3 because her coefficients match. The
    reduced pair is:

      5p2 + 3p3 = 37
       p2 +  p3 =  9

    p1 is gone from both. Two equations, two unknowns.
  - Repeat the move on the first of the pair. Isolate p2 by
    subtracting 3p3 and dividing by 5:

      p2 = 37/5 - (3/5)p3

  - Substitute into the last equation: 37/5 - (3/5)p3 + p3 = 9.
    Combine the p3 terms (1 - 3/5 = 2/5) and move 37/5 across
    (9 - 37/5 = 8/5):

      (2/5)p3 = 8/5

  - Multiply both sides by 5/2 and p3 = 4. A muffin costs 4. The
    lecture's version of this step carries much larger fractions.
    The moves are identical, and the professor invites you to
    double check his math. Do the same with your own.
  - What just happened, in plain terms (added explanation): every
    substitution used one shopper's receipt to eliminate one price
    from everybody else's receipt. Three receipts, three prices,
    three rounds, and the last receipt standing has only one price
    left in it.

6. WORKING BACK UP TO THE PRICES
  - Now cash in the formulas from the bottom up. Put p3 = 4 into
    the formula for p2:

      p2 = 37/5 - (3/5)(4) = 37/5 - 12/5 = 25/5 = 5

    A latte costs 5.
  - Put p2 = 5 and p3 = 4 into the formula for p1:

      p1 = 15/2 - (1/2)(5) - (1/2)(4) = 15/2 - 5/2 - 4/2 = 6/2 = 3

    An espresso costs 3.
  - So p = [3, 5, 4]. The lecture closes the loop by plugging the
    solution back into the original system and confirming every
    row adds up. Do the same here: Dana 2(3) + 5 + 4 = 15, Eli
    3 + 3(5) + 2(4) = 26, Faye 3 + 2(5) + 2(4) = 21. All three
    receipts match.
  - The point of the check (added explanation): substitution
    involves a lot of fraction arithmetic, and one slipped sign
    poisons everything below it. Plugging the answer back into the
    ORIGINAL equations, not the reduced ones, catches that. It is
    cheap and it is the same habit as re-running a test after a
    fix.

7. CHECKING THE ANSWER IN PYTHON
  - The lecture's second check uses NumPy. Define the matrix as a
    NumPy array, define the solution vector, take the dot product,
    and compare against the cost vector. It matches the right-hand
    side of every equation.
  - Same check for the coffee shop:

      import numpy as np
      Q = np.array([[2, 1, 1], [1, 3, 2], [1, 2, 2]]) ## rows = customers
      p = np.array([3, 5, 4]) ## espresso, latte, muffin
      print(Q.dot(p)) ## expect [15 26 21]

    Output: [15 26 21]. The dot product of the matrix with the
    price vector reproduces the receipts, which is exactly the
    statement M p = c that the whole lecture is built on.
  - Note what this does and does not do (added explanation). It
    verifies a solution you already found. It does not find one.
    Finding the solution in code is Lecture 4's job, and the
    algorithm behind it is Lecture 2's.

8. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - A matrix can hold data (rows of shoppers, columns of products)
    and at the same time act as a function that turns a price
    vector into a cost vector: M p = c.
  - Writing out the row-by-row dot products of M p = c produces a
    system of linear equations, one equation per row.
  - Three unknowns need three equations.
  - Solve by substitution: isolate one unknown as a formula in the
    others, substitute it into the remaining equations, repeat
    until one equation has one unknown, solve it, then substitute
    numbers back up the chain.
  - Always verify by plugging the solution into the original
    equations, by hand or with a NumPy dot product.

9. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - In the lecture's matrix, columns represent grocery items and
    rows represent the individuals purchasing.
  - The known quantities are the matrix M (purchases) and the cost
    vector c (totals paid). The unknown is the price vector p.
  - The relationship is M acting on p equals c.
  - Each row of the matrix, dotted with p, gives one linear
    equation. The full set is a system of linear equations.
  - The lecture's system has three equations and three unknowns.
  - Strategy: take one variable at a time, find a formula for it in
    terms of the other unknowns, substitute that formula into the
    subsequent equations, group like terms, and repeat.
  - After the forward pass, the last equation contains only one
    unknown and can be solved as a number.
  - Solved values are substituted back up through the earlier
    equations to recover the remaining unknowns.
  - The result is checked by substituting the solution into the
    system, and again in Python by taking the NumPy dot product of
    the matrix and the solution vector and comparing to the cost
    vector.
