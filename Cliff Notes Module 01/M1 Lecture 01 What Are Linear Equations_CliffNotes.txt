1. WHAT THIS LECTURE COVERS
  - The professor's opening definition: linear algebra is the
    study of linear systems and linear equations. He calls it a
    circular definition and says it is not much help until you
    see examples of what "linear," "equation," and "system" mean.
  - The plan for the module: walk through example scenarios of
    linear systems, see how they capture real-world problems,
    then define the tools (vectors, matrices, and so on) that
    make up linear algebra.
  - This lecture builds one scenario, the fruit stand, and uses
    it to introduce four things: the linear equation, the
    summation shorthand, solving for one variable in terms of
    another, and the slope-intercept form of a line.
  - Practical framing (added): a linear equation is the simplest
    possible model of a constraint. A budget, a capacity, a
    weight limit. If you have ever written a spreadsheet cell
    that multiplies quantities by unit prices and sums them, you
    have written one.

2. THE FRUIT STAND SCENARIO
  - The setup as the professor gives it: you are sent to a fruit
    stand with $30. You must spend all $30. Apples cost $2 each
    and oranges cost $3 each. Which combinations of apples and
    oranges use up the budget exactly?
  - Two simplifications he insists on. First, you must spend all
    the money, not some of it. Second, fractional groceries are
    allowed, so one and a half apples is a legal purchase. He
    calls the whole thing "very contrived" and made up. The
    fractional rule is what makes the math clean.
  - Why the rules matter (added): "spend all of it" turns an
    inequality (spend at most $30) into an equation (spend
    exactly $30). "Fractions allowed" means the variables live on
    a continuous number line instead of on the integers. Both
    rules are there so that the answer is a line, not a scatter
    of dots.
  - His first pass at writing the scenario down is the long form:
    $30 = ($2 x apples) + ($3 x oranges), where the italic words
    apples and oranges stand for the number purchased.

3. VARIABLES AND NOTATION
  - KEY TERM: variable: a symbol standing for some number or some
    other mathematical object. The lecture writes variables in
    italics. Sometimes a full word (apples), sometimes a single
    letter (x or y are the common ones). The italics are the hint
    that you are looking at a variable rather than a label.
  - He then strips the equation down. Three moves:
    - The multiplication x becomes a dot. "2 . apples" reads as
      two times the number of apples.
    - The dollar signs go away. It is understood that 30 means
      $30 and 2 means $2 per apple.
    - The parentheses go away.
  - Result: 30 = 2.apples + 3.oranges. Fewer characters, same
    equation. He is explicit that this is mathematically the
    same statement as the long form.
  - Why he bothers (added): the notation shrink is not
    cosmetic. Every later object in this module (summations,
    vectors, dot products, matrices) is a further compression of
    the same "number times variable, added up" pattern. Getting
    comfortable with the terse form now makes the later
    shorthands feel like the next obvious step instead of a new
    language.

4. THE GENERAL LINEAR EQUATION
  - KEY TERM: linear equation: any equation of the form
    y = a_1.x_1 + a_2.x_2 + a_3.x_3 + ... + a_N.x_N.
    The left side is one value. The right side is as many terms
    as you like, every one of them a number times a variable,
    all added together.
  - Mapping the fruit stand onto the pattern:
    - y is the total, the $30 budget. y = 30.
    - x_1 is the number of apples, x_2 is the number of oranges.
    - a_1 is the cost of item 1 ($2 per apple), a_2 is the cost
      of item 2 ($3 per orange).
  - The pattern scales. More grocery items means more
    number-times-variable terms, one per item. Nothing else
    changes.
  - What "linear" rules out (added): no variable is squared, no
    two variables are multiplied together, no variable sits
    inside a square root or a sine. Each variable appears once,
    multiplied by a plain number. That is the whole restriction,
    and it is why the graph comes out straight.
  - The x_i and a_i notation (added): the subscript i is just an
    index, a slot number. x_3 means "the third variable," a_3
    means "the number multiplying the third variable." When the
    professor says "these a's" and "these x's" he means the
    whole family of them.

5. THE SUMMATION SHORTHAND
  - The right side of the general equation repeats one pattern:
    a number times a variable, plus a number times a variable,
    and so on. The professor introduces "this big Epsilon thing"
    (the capital sigma symbol, added) as a way to write the
    repetition once.
  - KEY TERM: summation: the sigma notation that says "add up the
    following expression for every value of i from a start to an
    end." y = sum from i=1 to N of a_i.x_i.
  - How to read it, in his words: start with i=1, that gives you
    a_1.x_1. Then i=2 gives a_2.x_2. Then i=3 gives a_3.x_3. Keep
    going up to i=N, which gives a_N.x_N. Add all of them. The
    big long equation from section 4 is now one short line.
  - He says this notation will come back to represent big
    equations in a small space.
  - Example (added): a coffee cart sells bagels at $3 and coffees
    at $4, and you have exactly $24 to spend. The linear equation
    is 24 = 3.bagels + 4.coffees. In summation form, with x_1 =
    bagels, x_2 = coffees, a_1 = 3, a_2 = 4, and N = 2, it is
    24 = sum from i=1 to 2 of a_i.x_i. Unrolling: i=1 gives
    3.bagels, i=2 gives 4.coffees, sum them, done. For two items
    the sigma saves nothing. For two hundred it saves a page.
  - Code (added): the summation is a loop, and NumPy's sum of an
    elementwise product is the same loop without writing it.
      import numpy as np
      a,x = np.array([3,4]),np.array([4,3]) ## prices, counts
      print(np.sum(a*x)) ## 24, the unrolled summation

6. HOW MANY SOLUTIONS, AND REARRANGING THE EQUATION
  - The question: how many apple-and-orange combinations satisfy
    30 = 2.apples + 3.oranges? The answer: infinitely many.
  - The reason you can see it is by rewriting the equation with
    a little algebra. His rule for algebra, stated plainly:
    whatever you do to one side of the equals sign, you must do
    to the other side, or the two sides stop being equal.
  - His two steps:
    - Subtract 2.apples from both sides. Now the apples term is
      on the left with the 30.
    - Divide both sides by 3. The 30 becomes 30/3, which is 10.
      The 2.apples becomes 2/3.apples. The 3.oranges becomes
      oranges, because 3/3 is 1 and the 1 is not written.
  - Result: oranges = 10 - 2/3.apples. Tell him the number of
    apples and this equation tells you the number of oranges
    that spends the budget exactly.
  - Why infinitely many (added): apples can be any number from 0
    up to 15 (fractions included), and each choice produces
    exactly one oranges value. A continuous range of inputs
    gives a continuous range of solutions. Infinite.
  - His check: buy 3 apples and the equation says 8 oranges.
    3 apples at $2 is $6, 8 oranges at $3 is $24, total $30.
  - Example (added): the coffee cart again, 24 = 3.bagels +
    4.coffees. Solve for coffees the same way.
    - Subtract 3.bagels from both sides: 24 - 3.bagels =
      4.coffees.
    - Divide both sides by 4: 6 - 3/4.bagels = coffees.
    - Check with 4 bagels: 6 - 3/4 of 4 = 6 - 3 = 3 coffees.
      4 bagels at $3 is $12, 3 coffees at $4 is $12. Total $24.
    - Check with 0 bagels: 6 coffees, $24. Check with 8 bagels:
      0 coffees, $24. Every bagel count in between works too.
  - Practical reading (added): "oranges depends on apples" is
    the sentence to hold onto. The professor says it is because
    the budget is fixed, must be fully spent, and fractions are
    allowed. Rearranging an equation so one variable sits alone
    on the left is the same move you make when you solve any
    formula for the unknown you care about. Nothing new, just
    named.

7. SLOPE AND INTERCEPT
  - The rearranged equation has the general form y = b + m.x.
    The professor says this is a really common way of
    representing a straight line.
  - KEY TERM: slope: the m in y = b + m.x. It tells you how y
    changes when x changes a little bit.
  - KEY TERM: intercept: the b in y = b + m.x. It tells you what
    y is when x is zero. Set x to zero, the m.x term is zero, and
    b is what is left over.
  - For the fruit stand, m = -2/3 and b = 10. His reading of
    each: m says that every additional apple means 2/3 of an
    orange less. b says that with zero apples you buy 10 oranges
    at $3 each to use up the $30.
  - Example (added): the coffee cart, coffees = 6 - 3/4.bagels.
    - m = -3/4. Each extra bagel costs you three quarters of a
      coffee.
    - b = 6. Zero bagels means six coffees, $24 exactly.
    - Sanity check on the sign: the slope is negative because
      the budget is fixed. More of one thing forces less of the
      other. A positive slope would mean buying more bagels
      somehow let you buy more coffee too, which a fixed budget
      cannot do.
  - Why m is a fraction of the prices (added): m = -(price of
    x) / (price of y). Each bagel eats $3 of budget, and each $4
    of budget is one coffee, so one bagel displaces 3/4 of a
    coffee. The slope is the exchange rate between the two
    items. Same for the fruit stand: -2/3 is $2 per apple over
    $3 per orange.

8. WHAT "LINEAR" MEANS
  - Plot the equation with apples on the horizontal axis and
    oranges on the vertical axis and you get a straight line.
    That is what linear means in this example. See the plot and
    the slider widget in Lecture 1 (notebook section "Example 1:
    The Fruitstand," the visualize function).
  - The widget moves a point along the line as you change the
    number of apples. His observation: for each new apple, the
    allowed oranges drop by about 2/3. The line is straight
    because that drop is the same everywhere along it.
  - The test for linearity in one sentence (added): the change
    in y for one step in x is the same no matter where you
    start. Constant slope means straight line means linear.
  - Code (added): reproduce the shape with the coffee cart.
      import numpy as np
      import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
      b = np.linspace(0,8,50) ## bagel counts, 0 to 8
      plt.plot(b,6-0.75*b) ## coffees as a line
      plt.xlabel("bagels")
      plt.ylabel("coffees")

9. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Linear algebra is the study of linear systems and linear
    equations. The definition is circular, the examples are the
    real definition.
  - A linear equation is y = a_1.x_1 + a_2.x_2 + ... + a_N.x_N.
    Numbers times variables, added up. That pattern is the
    entire subject in miniature.
  - The sigma summation is the shorthand for that repeating
    pattern.
  - A single linear equation in two unknowns has infinitely many
    solutions. Rearrange it to see one variable as a function of
    the other.
  - Whatever you do to one side of the equals sign, do to the
    other.
  - y = b + m.x is a straight line. m is the slope (how y moves
    per unit of x), b is the intercept (y when x is zero).
  - Linear means the plot is a straight line.

  - Plotting apples against oranges gives a straight line, hence
