1. WHAT THIS LECTURE COVERS
  - The method for finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a square
    matrix by hand, built up one piece at a time: the identity
    matrix, a rearranged definition, the determinant as an area,
    a quadratic equation, and finally the eigenvectors from the
    eigenvalues.
  - This is the heaviest lecture of the module. The mechanics are
    all 2 by 2 arithmetic. The hard part is the one idea in the
    middle, "somehow equal to zero", and that gets its own section.

2. THE STRATEGY: EIGENVALUES FIRST
  - Recall the definition. v is an eigenvector of M if M . v
    rescales v by a scalar lambda without changing its angle.
  - For an n by n matrix M there will be n eigenvectors and n
    corresponding eigenvalues. Subscripts pair them: v1 goes with
    lambda1, v2 with lambda2, and so on.
  - The plan in general: first find all the eigenvalues, all the
    lambdas. Then use each eigenvalue to find its eigenvector
    through the rescaling relationship.
  - Why in that order (added): the definition has two unknowns in
    it, v and lambda, and you cannot solve for both at once. The
    trick below removes v from the picture, leaving an equation in
    lambda alone. Solve that, then put v back.

3. THE IDENTITY MATRIX
  - KEY TERM: identity matrix: a square n by n matrix, written I,
    with ones along the main diagonal from the top left to the
    bottom right and zeros everywhere else.
        I (3 by 3) = [[1, 0, 0],
                      [0, 1, 0],
                      [0, 0, 1]]
  - Why "identity": if I acts on any matrix M or any vector v, you
    just get that matrix or vector back on the other side.
  - Example (added): I . (7, -2) = (1*7 + 0*(-2), 0*7 + 1*(-2)) =
    (7, -2). And with A = [[4,2],[1,3]], both I . A and A . I come
    back as A. It is the matrix version of multiplying by one.
  - Why it is needed here (added): the eigenvector equation has a
    matrix on one side and a plain number on the other. To subtract
    one from the other you need the number dressed up as a matrix.
    lambda times I is that dressed-up number.

4. REARRANGING THE DEFINITION
  - Start from M . v = lambda * v. Subtract M . v from both sides.
    The left side becomes zero, really a vector full of zeros, and
    the right side becomes lambda * v - M . v.
  - Both terms contain v, so pull it out front as if v were a
    number. What is left is (lambda * I - M) acting on v, and the
    whole thing must equal the vector of zeros.
        (lambda * I - M) . v = 0
  - The identity matrix is what makes "pull v out" legal (added).
    lambda * v is the same as (lambda * I) . v, and now both terms
    are a matrix times v, so the matrices can be combined.

5. THE TRIVIAL SOLUTION AND WHY IT IS IGNORED
  - There are two ways that equation can be true. The first is that
    v is the zero vector. That works for any matrix M, every time.
  - KEY TERM: trivial solution: the eigenvector of all zeros. It
    always satisfies the equation and it says nothing about M
    compared to any other matrix, so it is ignored.
  - If v is not the zero vector, then the stuff in the parentheses,
    lambda * I - M, must somehow be equal to zero.
  - Why that is strange: lambda * I is an n by n matrix, M is an
    n by n matrix, their difference is still an n by n matrix, and
    yet acting on a nonzero v it has to produce the zero vector.
    It does not have to be a matrix full of zeros. It has to be
    zero in a different sense.

6. "SOMEHOW EQUAL TO ZERO": THE UNIT SQUARE
  - The sense that matters: does M, as a function, stretch the x-y
    plane in a way that shrinks space so that the resulting area is
    zero? The lecture's own words are "I know this is a bit weird
    to think about", and then it gives a picture. Here is the
    picture in numbers.
  - KEY TERM: unit square: the square with base and height both 1
    and its bottom left corner at the origin. Its corners are
    (0,0), (0,1), (1,1), and (1,0).
  - Write those corners as columns of a 2 by 4 matrix S:
        S = [[0, 0, 1, 1],
             [0, 1, 1, 0]]
  - Keep M generic, M = [[a, b], [c, d]], and let it act on S. Row
    one of M dotted with each column of S, then row two:
        M . S = [[0, b, a + b, a],
                 [0, d, c + d, c]]
    First column: (a,b) . (0,0) is 0. Second: (a,b) . (0,1) is b.
    Third: (a,b) . (1,1) is a + b. Same again with row (c,d).
  - The result still has four corners and it is square-like, but
    instead of the unit square it defines a parallelogram, a
    rectangle warped a little. Different choices of a, b, c, d give
    different parallelograms with different areas. Sometimes the
    area is zero, and that is the case being hunted.
  - Example (added), M = [[3, 1], [1, 2]]:
        M . S = [[0, 1, 4, 3],
                 [0, 2, 3, 1]]
    Corners (0,0), (1,2), (4,3), (3,1). A tilted parallelogram
    with a nonzero area.
  - Example (added), M = [[2, 4], [1, 2]]:
        M . S = [[0, 4, 6, 2],
                 [0, 2, 3, 1]]
    Corners (0,0), (4,2), (6,3), (2,1). Every one of those points
    sits on the line y = x/2. The square has been flattened onto a
    single line. A flat parallelogram has area zero. This matrix
    is "somehow equal to zero" in the sense the lecture means: not
    a matrix of zeros, but a function that squashes a whole plane
    down to a line.
  - See the parallelogram widget with its a, b, c, d dials in the
    Lecture 9 notebook section "Finding Eigenvalues and
    Eigenvectors". With a = 1, d = 1, b = c = 0 it is the identity
    and you get the square back. Sliding a stretches horizontally
    into a rectangle, sliding d stretches vertically, and b and c
    produce the slanted parallelograms.

7. THE DETERMINANT
  - With some work you can recover the geometry and show that the
    area of the resulting parallelogram is a*d - b*c, using nothing
    but the four numbers in M.
  - Sanity check the lecture gives: when b and c are zero the
    parallelogram is a rectangle, and rectangle area is base times
    height, which is a*d. The b*c term is the correction for the
    slant once the shape is no longer a rectangle.
  - KEY TERM: determinant: for a 2 by 2 matrix M, the area of the
    parallelogram that M produces when it acts on the unit square.
        det(M) = m11 * m22 - m12 * m21
    Subscripts are row then column, so m12 is row 1, column 2.
  - Checking the examples (added): for [[3,1],[1,2]] the determinant
    is 3*2 - 1*1 = 5, and measuring the parallelogram with corners
    (0,0), (1,2), (4,3), (3,1) gives area 5. For [[2,4],[1,2]] the
    determinant is 2*2 - 4*1 = 0, matching the flattened square.
    For the rectangle [[2,0],[0,3]] it is 6, base 2 times height 3.
  - Code (added):
      import numpy as np
      for M in ([[3,1],[1,2]],[[2,4],[1,2]],[[2,0],[0,3]]):
          print(np.linalg.det(np.array(M)).round(4)) ## 5.0, 0.0, 6.0

8. BIGGER MATRICES
  - For a 3 by 3 matrix, the same approach applies with a unit cube
    instead of a unit square. M acts on the corners of the cube,
    the result is a three dimensional parallelogram, and the
    determinant is its volume. Zero volume is the case of interest.
  - The lecture gives the 3 by 3 formula. For M with rows (a,b,c),
    (d,e,f), (g,h,i):
        det(M) = aei + bfg + cdh - ceg - bdi - afh
  - Example (added): M = [[1,2,0],[0,3,1],[2,0,1]].
      aei = 1*3*1 = 3    bfg = 2*1*2 = 4    cdh = 0*0*0 = 0
      ceg = 0*3*2 = 0    bdi = 2*0*1 = 0    afh = 1*1*0 = 0
      det = 3 + 4 + 0 - 0 - 0 - 0 = 7
    NumPy's det agrees to floating point noise: it prints
    6.999999999999998 rather than 7.
  - For 4 by 4, 5 by 5, and up, it is the same idea with whatever
    the higher dimensional equivalent of volume is. The equations
    get very long. The lecture says you can certainly sort that out
    in higher dimensions, and then returns to 2 by 2 for the rest.
  - Practical note (added): nobody expands a 5 by 5 by hand.
    np.linalg.det does it. The formula matters for understanding
    what the number means and for the 2 by 2 case on a quiz.

9. THE CHARACTERISTIC EQUATION IS A QUADRATIC
  - Back to 2 by 2. The real question is: when is the determinant
    of (lambda * I - M) equal to zero?
  - Multiply through. lambda * I puts lambda on the diagonal and
    zeros off it. Subtract M component-wise:
        lambda * I - M = [[lambda - m11,  -m12       ],
                          [-m21,          lambda - m22]]
  - Apply the 2 by 2 determinant, ad - bc, and be careful with the
    minus signs:
        0 = (lambda - m11)(lambda - m22) - (-m12)(-m21)
        0 = lambda^2 - lambda(m11 + m22) + (m11*m22 - m12*m21)
  - Look at the lambdas. A scalar times lambda squared, minus a
    scalar times lambda, plus a term with no lambda in it. This is
    just a quadratic equation, and quadratic equations have a
    formula.
  - The lecture writes the leading coefficient as (1) times lambda
    squared. Multiplying by one changes nothing, it just makes the
    "a" of the quadratic formula visible.
  - Two shortcuts worth memorizing (added): m11 + m22 is the sum of
    the diagonal, and m11*m22 - m12*m21 is det(M). So the quadratic
    is always lambda^2 - (diagonal sum) lambda + det(M) = 0 for a
    2 by 2. Two numbers off the matrix and you have the equation.

10. THE QUADRATIC FORMULA
  - KEY TERM: quadratic formula: for a*x^2 + b*x + c = 0,
        x = (-b plus or minus sqrt(b^2 - 4ac)) / 2a
  - Match the parts. Here a is 1, b is -(m11 + m22), and c is
    m11*m22 - m12*m21. Plug in:
        lambda = ((m11 + m22) plus or minus
                  sqrt((m11 + m22)^2 - 4(1)(m11*m22 - m12*m21)))
                 / 2(1)
  - The plus or minus yields two different eigenvalues, lambda1
    with the plus and lambda2 with the minus. Two eigenvalues for a
    2 by 2, as promised in section 2.
  - Example (added), M = [[4, 2], [1, 3]]:
      diagonal sum = 4 + 3 = 7
      det(M) = 4*3 - 2*1 = 10
      0 = lambda^2 - 7 lambda + 10
      lambda = (7 plus or minus sqrt(49 - 40)) / 2
             = (7 plus or minus 3) / 2
      lambda1 = 5, lambda2 = 2
    Check against NumPy: np.linalg.eigvals([[4,2],[1,3]]) prints
    [5. 2.].
  - Example (added), the symmetric matrix from Lecture 8,
    M = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]: diagonal sum 4, determinant 3, so
    lambda^2 - 4 lambda + 3 = 0, and lambda = (4 plus or minus
    sqrt(16 - 12)) / 2 = (4 plus or minus 2) / 2, giving 3 and 1.
    Those are the two eigenvalues found by inspection in the
    Lecture 8 notes.

11. FROM EIGENVALUE TO EIGENVECTOR
  - Now find v1, the eigenvector for lambda1. Call its components a
    and b, so v1 = (a, b). The question is: what are a and b?
  - What is known: lambda1 is a number from the quadratic formula,
    and M acting on v1 equals v1 rescaled by lambda1. Write the
    left side out as two row dot products:
        [[m11, m12],  . [a,   =  [lambda1 * a,
         [m21, m22]]     b]       lambda1 * b]
      m11*a + m12*b = lambda1 * a
      m21*a + m22*b = lambda1 * b
  - Treat those as two separate equations. Subtract lambda1*a from
    both sides of the first and lambda1*b from both sides of the
    second:
      0 = (m11 - lambda1)*a + m12*b
      0 = m21*a + (m22 - lambda1)*b
    Both must be true at once.
  - The trivial solution shows up again: a = 0 and b = 0 works for
    any matrix. Ignored for the same reason as before.
  - Focus on the first equation. Subtract m12*b from both sides,
    divide both sides by (m11 - lambda1), and move the minus sign
    into the denominator:
        a = (m12 / (lambda1 - m11)) * b
    Now a is written as a function of b. Plug it into v1:
        v1 = ( (m12 / (lambda1 - m11)) * b,  b )
  - Continuing the example (added), M = [[4, 2], [1, 3]] with
    lambda1 = 5:
      a = (2 / (5 - 4)) * b = 2b
      v1 = (2b, b). Pick b = 1: v1 = (2, 1).
      Check: M . (2, 1) = (4*2 + 2*1, 1*2 + 3*1) = (10, 5) = 5 * (2, 1)
    And for lambda2 = 2:
      a = (2 / (2 - 4)) * b = -b
      v2 = (-b, b). Pick b = 1: v2 = (-1, 1).
      Check: M . (-1, 1) = (-4 + 2, -1 + 3) = (-2, 2) = 2 * (-1, 1)
    Both checks land exactly on lambda times the vector. That check
    is the whole definition, so if it passes, you are done.

12. INFINITELY MANY EIGENVECTORS PER EIGENVALUE
  - At this point the method is stuck on b. There is no equation
    that pins b down.
  - That is not a flaw. There are infinitely many eigenvectors
    associated with a given eigenvalue, and you find all of them by
    varying b. Every one of them is aligned with the others: same
    angle, different length, rescaled versions of each other.
  - Whatever b you choose, M acting on that vector gives back a
    version scaled by lambda1. In the example, (2,1), (4,2), and
    (-6,-3) are all eigenvectors for lambda1 = 5, because they all
    point along the same line (added).
  - The lecture's demo lets you tune m12, m11, and lambda1 and then
    slide b. Every vector produced lines up along one axis. See the
    v1 slider widget at the end of the Lecture 9 notebook section.
  - What software does about it (added): NumPy picks the one with
    length 1. Lecture 10 covers that.

13. THE WHOLE METHOD ON ONE PAGE (added)
  - Given a 2 by 2 matrix M:
      1. Diagonal sum = m11 + m22. Determinant = m11*m22 - m12*m21.
      2. Solve lambda^2 - (diagonal sum) lambda + det = 0 with the
         quadratic formula. Two roots, lambda1 and lambda2.
      3. For each lambda: a = (m12 / (lambda - m11)) * b, pick b = 1,
         eigenvector is (m12 / (lambda - m11), 1). Scale to taste.
      4. Check: M . v should equal lambda * v.
  - Code (added):
      import numpy as np
      M = np.array([[4,2],[1,3]])
      tr,det = M[0,0]+M[1,1],np.linalg.det(M) ## diagonal sum, determinant
      lams = ((tr+np.sqrt(tr**2-4*det))/2,(tr-np.sqrt(tr**2-4*det))/2) ## quadratic formula
      for lam in lams: v = np.array([M[0,1]/(lam-M[0,0]),1]); print(lam.round(6), v, np.dot(M,v), lam*v) ## last two match
  - One edge to know about (added): if lambda - m11 is zero the
    division fails. Use the second equation instead, solved for b
    in terms of a. Same idea, other row.

14. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Strategy: eigenvalues first, then eigenvectors.
  - The identity matrix I has ones on the main diagonal and returns
    whatever it acts on unchanged.
  - M . v = lambda * v rearranges to (lambda * I - M) . v = 0. The
    zero vector is the trivial solution and is ignored.
  - "Somehow equal to zero" means the matrix squashes the plane to
    zero area. The determinant measures that area: ad - bc for a
    2 by 2.
  - det(lambda * I - M) = 0 is a quadratic in lambda. The quadratic
    formula gives the two eigenvalues.
  - For each eigenvalue, the first row equation gives a in terms of
    b. Any b works, all choices lie on the same line.

15. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - An n by n matrix has n eigenvectors and n eigenvalues.
  - The identity matrix: square, ones along the main diagonal from
    top left to bottom right, zeros elsewhere. I acting on M or v
    returns M or v.
  - Subtracting M . v from both sides of the definition gives the
    zero vector on the left and (lambda * I - M) acting on v on the
    right.
  - The zero vector is the trivial solution, true for any M, and
    ignored.
  - The unit square has corners (0,0), (0,1), (1,1), (1,0), written
    as the columns of a 2 by 4 matrix S. M = [[a,b],[c,d]] acting on
    S gives [[0, b, a+b, a], [0, d, c+d, c]], a parallelogram.
  - Area of that parallelogram, and the determinant of a 2 by 2:
    a*d - b*c, or m11*m22 - m12*m21.
  - For a 3 by 3, the determinant is the volume after M acts on the
    unit cube: aei + bfg + cdh - ceg - bdi - afh.
  - det(lambda * I - M) expands to lambda^2 - lambda(m11 + m22) +
    (m11*m22 - m12*m21), a quadratic.
  - Quadratic formula: x = (-b plus or minus sqrt(b^2 - 4ac)) / 2a.
  - The plus or minus gives two eigenvalues, lambda1 and lambda2.
  - Eigenvector for lambda1: a = (m12 / (lambda1 - m11)) * b, so
    v1 = ((m12 / (lambda1 - m11)) * b, b) for any b.
  - There are infinitely many eigenvectors per eigenvalue, all with
    the same angle and different lengths.
