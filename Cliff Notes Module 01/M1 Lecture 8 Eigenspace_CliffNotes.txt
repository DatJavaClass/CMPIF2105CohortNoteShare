1. WHAT THIS LECTURE COVERS
  - The idea of an eigenvector: a vector that a matrix does not
    rotate. And its eigenvalue: how much the matrix stretches or
    shrinks that vector.
  - Why anyone cares: for symmetric matrices, the eigenvectors form
    a new coordinate system, the eigenspace, that describes data in
    its natural coordinates. Lecture 9 shows how to find them,
    Lecture 11 shows what they buy you.

2. THE OBSERVATION THAT STARTS IT
  - Sometimes it is helpful to find the vectors that remain
    unchanged by a linear transformation. The lecture shows this
    with the shear matrix from Lecture 7.
  - Recap of the setup: each choice of a and b gives a different
    matrix M, and M acts on the matrix of scatter points by the
    matrix dot product. Varying a shears horizontally, varying b
    shears vertically.
  - The observation: as b varies, M changes every time the slider
    moves, but the blue reference vector does not change. Its angle
    stays put no matter what b is. See the shear widget in the
    Lecture 8 notebook section "Eigenvectors & Eigenvalues" and
    watch the blue arrow while moving b.
  - That is the type of thing being hunted: a vector such that when
    the matrix acts on it, the vector does not rotate at all.

3. THE DEFINITION
  - KEY TERM: eigenvector: a vector v is an eigenvector of the
    matrix M if v does not rotate when M acts on it. The dot
    product of M and v is a new vector with the same exact angle
    as v. It can shrink or grow along that original axis, but it
    cannot turn.
  - Written as an equation:
        M . v = lambda * v
    The left side is M acting on v. The right side is v scaled by
    some number lambda.
  - KEY TERM: eigenvalue: the scalar lambda in that equation. It is
    the amount of shrinking or growing that happens to v when M
    acts on v. Lambda is the eigenvalue corresponding to the
    eigenvector v.
  - Reading the equation in words (added): "M does to v exactly
    what multiplying by the number lambda does." A whole grid of
    numbers collapses to one number, but only for that special
    direction.
  - Example (added), a matrix that stretches x by 3 and y by 2:
        M = [[3, 0],
             [0, 2]]
      M . (1, 0) = (3, 0)  which is 3 * (1, 0)
      M . (0, 1) = (0, 2)  which is 2 * (0, 1)
      M . (1, 1) = (3, 2)  which is not any number times (1, 1)
    So (1,0) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3, (0,1) is an
    eigenvector with eigenvalue 2, and (1,1) is not an eigenvector
    at all: it came out pointing somewhere new. Two directions
    survive untouched, every other direction gets turned (added).
  - Code (added):
      import numpy as np
      M = np.array([[3,0],[0,2]])
      for v in ([1,0],[0,1],[1,1]): print(v,'->',np.dot(M,v)) ## which ones keep their angle
  - Eigenvalues can be negative or fractional (added). Negative
    means the vector flips to point the opposite way along the same
    line. It still satisfies M . v = lambda * v, so it is still an
    eigenvector: the line it lives on is unchanged, only the length
    and the sign moved. Lecture 10's example has one. A fraction
    below one means it shrinks.

4. SYMMETRIC MATRICES
  - Certain types of matrices yield eigenvectors and eigenvalues
    with really useful properties for describing the matrix as a
    function. The lecture's case is the symmetric matrix.
  - KEY TERM: symmetric matrix: the top right triangle of the
    matrix is the mirror image of the bottom left triangle. Draw a
    line from the top left corner to the bottom right corner, flip
    the top right triangle over that line, and it lines up with the
    bottom left.
  - The lecture's picture is a 4 by 4 with letters, where the same
    letter appears in mirrored positions on either side of the
    diagonal. See the colored 4 by 4 in the Lecture 8 notebook
    right after the eigenvector definition.
  - Example (added), a 3 by 3 that is symmetric:
        [[1, 4, 7],
         [4, 2, 9],
         [7, 9, 3]]
    The 4 sits at row 1 column 2 and again at row 2 column 1. The 7
    and the 9 mirror the same way. The diagonal (1, 2, 3) can be
    anything, it is its own mirror image. Change the bottom left 7
    to an 8 and the matrix is no longer symmetric (added).
  - Practical framing (added): symmetric matrices show up whenever
    a grid records a relationship that runs both ways. A distance
    table between cities is symmetric because the distance from A
    to B equals the distance from B to A. The covariance matrix in
    Lecture 11 is symmetric for the same reason.

5. THE PAYOFF: PERPENDICULAR EIGENVECTORS
  - A symmetric n by n matrix will have n unique eigenvectors, and
    they will all be mutually perpendicular to each other.
  - Put concretely: if v1 and v2 are different eigenvectors of a
    symmetric matrix M, they are oriented 90 degrees away from each
    other.
  - Another way to say the same thing: take the dot product of any
    two of M's eigenvectors and it is zero, because they are 90
    degrees apart. This is the Lecture 5 rule that perpendicular
    vectors have a cosine of zero, applied to eigenvectors (added).
  - Example (added), symmetric 2 by 2:
        M = [[2, 1],
             [1, 2]]
      M . (1, 1)  = (3, 3)  = 3 * (1, 1)     eigenvalue 3
      M . (1, -1) = (1, -1) = 1 * (1, -1)    eigenvalue 1
      (1, 1) . (1, -1) = 1 - 1 = 0            perpendicular
    Two eigenvectors, a dot product of zero, a right angle between
    them. Exactly what the rule promises for a symmetric matrix.
  - Counterexample (added), not symmetric:
        M = [[2, 1],
             [0, 3]]
      M . (1, 0) = (2, 0) = 2 * (1, 0)     eigenvalue 2
      M . (1, 1) = (3, 3) = 3 * (1, 1)     eigenvalue 3
      (1, 0) . (1, 1) = 1                  not perpendicular
    Two perfectly good eigenvectors, 45 degrees apart, dot product
    of one. The perpendicular guarantee is a symmetric-matrix
    guarantee and nothing more.
  - Code (added):
      import numpy as np
      S,T = np.array([[2,1],[1,2]]),np.array([[2,1],[0,3]])
      for M in (S,T):
          vals,vecs = np.linalg.eig(M) ## columns of vecs are eigenvectors
          print(vals, np.dot(vecs[:,0],vecs[:,1])) ## 0.0 for S, 0.707 for T
    Lecture 10 covers np.linalg.eig properly. For now the point is
    the second number: zero for the symmetric matrix, not zero for
    the other one.

6. THE EIGENSPACE
  - The whole set of eigenvectors v1, v2, v3 up to vn effectively
    defines a new coordinate system, like the typical x-y plane but
    rotated or spun a little.
  - KEY TERM: eigenspace: that new coordinate system defined by the
    eigenvectors of M.
  - Why it is useful: it describes data in what are called the
    natural coordinates for that dataset.
  - Practical framing (added): the x and y axes are a choice, not a
    law. If your data is a cloud of points stretched along a
    diagonal, the natural axes are "along the stretch" and "across
    the stretch". For a symmetric matrix built from that data, the
    eigenvectors are those two axes, and the eigenvalues say how
    much stretch lives along each one. Lecture 11 uses exactly this
    to throw away the axes with almost no stretch.
  - In the example above, the eigenvectors (1,1) and (1,-1) are the
    x-y axes turned 45 degrees. That turned grid is the eigenspace
    of [[2,1],[1,2]] (added).

7. THE WARNING
  - These interpretations for eigenvectors are only true if M is
    symmetric. If M is not symmetric, or does not meet some other
    very specific requirements, then this may not be the case for
    its eigenvectors.
  - The counterexample in section 5 is the warning in numbers: a
    non-symmetric matrix still has eigenvectors, but they are not
    at right angles and do not make a clean rotated grid (added).
  - What survives for any square matrix (added): the definition
    itself. M . v = lambda * v still defines eigenvectors and
    eigenvalues, and Lecture 9's method still finds them. What you
    lose without symmetry is the guarantee of n perpendicular ones.

8. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - An eigenvector of M keeps its angle when M acts on it. It only
    stretches or shrinks. The factor is its eigenvalue lambda.
  - The equation: M . v = lambda * v.
  - A symmetric matrix mirrors across its main diagonal.
  - A symmetric n by n matrix has n eigenvectors that are all
    mutually perpendicular. Their pairwise dot products are zero.
  - Those eigenvectors define a rotated coordinate system called
    the eigenspace, the natural coordinates for the data.
  - Without symmetry, none of the perpendicular-grid guarantees
    apply.

9. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - Eigenvector: a vector that does not rotate when M acts on it.
    The result has the same exact angle as v, and can only shrink
    or grow along that axis.
  - Eigenvalue: the scalar lambda in M . v = lambda * v. It is the
    amount of shrinking or growing.
  - In the shear demo, the blue reference vector does not change as
    b varies. That is the motivating example.
  - Symmetric matrix: the top right triangle is the mirror image of
    the bottom left triangle across the top-left to bottom-right
    line.
  - A symmetric n by n matrix has n unique eigenvectors, all
    mutually perpendicular (90 degrees apart, dot product zero).
  - The set of eigenvectors defines a new coordinate system called
    the eigenspace, the natural coordinates for the dataset.
  - The perpendicular and coordinate-system interpretations hold
    only for symmetric matrices (or matrices meeting other very
    specific requirements).
