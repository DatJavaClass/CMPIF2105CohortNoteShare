1. WHAT THIS LECTURE COVERS
  - The vector dot product: what it is, why it exists, and two
    ways to compute it. The whole grocery equation collapses to
    three symbols by the end.
  - Along the way: the difference between the dot product and
    elementwise multiplication, the double meaning of the dot
    symbol, and the angle formula that explains what the dot
    product measures.
  - Practical framing (added): the dot product is "multiply
    matching pairs, then add." Price times quantity summed over
    a receipt. Weight times score summed over a rubric. It is
    the single most-used operation in this course, and every
    matrix product in Lectures 7 through 9 is a stack of them.

2. THE GROCERY PROBLEM IN VECTORS
  - Recap: $30 budget, combinations of grocery items with
    different prices, spend it all.
  - Two vectors, both of length 3. g encodes the amounts: first
    component apples, second oranges, third bread loaves. c
    encodes the costs: (2, 3, 5).
  - His question: how can g and c represent the budget
    equation? Look at the equation and what you see is each
    vector's components multiplied pairwise, then summed. First
    component of c times first component of g, plus second times
    second, plus third times third.

3. THE DOT PRODUCT DEFINED
  - This operation happens so often it gets a name.
  - KEY TERM: vector dot product: the sum of the componentwise
    products of two vectors. Written c . g with a small dot. In
    summation form, the sum over i of c_i times g_i.
  - The notation, in his words: the arrows on top say c and g
    are vectors. The subscript i says you are talking about the
    ith component of each, which is a number, not a vector.
    i = 1 gives the first components multiplied, i = 2 the
    second, and so on.
  - The payoff: the whole budget equation becomes 30 = c . g.
    "The same exact information," shortened again.
  - The result of a dot product is a single number (added). Two
    vectors go in, one scalar comes out. That is worth saying
    out loud because the next operation returns a vector, and
    mixing the two up is the standard beginner error.
  - Example (added): a coffee cart's cost vector is c =
    (3, 4, 2) for bagels, coffees, muffins. An order is g =
    (2, 1, 4).
    - Pairwise products: 3 times 2 = 6, 4 times 1 = 4, 2 times
      4 = 8.
    - Sum: 6 + 4 + 8 = 18.
    - c . g = 18. The order costs $18. That is the receipt total
      in one operation.
  - Why the definition is shaped this way (added): a linear
    equation is a sum of number-times-variable terms. Put the
    numbers in one vector and the variables in another and the
    equation is, by construction, a dot product. The dot product
    was not invented and then found useful. It is the linear
    equation with the two kinds of information pulled apart.

4. ONE DOT, TWO MEANINGS
  - He flags a confusion. The dot symbol is doing two jobs. On
    the left, c . g between two vectors means the vector dot
    product. On the right, c_i . g_i between two components
    means the usual multiplication of two numbers.
  - His words: "this double usage of notation, it's confusing,
    it's a little unfortunate."
  - How to keep it straight (added): look at what is on either
    side of the dot. Arrows on top, or a whole list?
    Vector dot product, result is a number. Subscripts, or plain
    numbers? Ordinary multiplication. In code there is no
    ambiguity: np.dot for the first, * between scalars for the
    second.

5. ELEMENTWISE MULTIPLICATION
  - The confusion raises a question: what about a more natural
    vector multiplication, one that produces a new vector by
    multiplying the components? That exists.
  - KEY TERM: elementwise multiplication: multiply the two
    vectors component by component and keep the results as a
    new vector of the same length. Written with an asterisk,
    c * g, to keep it apart from the dot product.
  - For c and g of length 3, c * g is a new vector of size 3
    whose entries are the three pairwise products.
  - His demo: two vectors a (red) and b (blue) with sliders for
    each x and y coordinate, and their elementwise product in
    black. See the visualizeElementProduct widget in Lecture 5
    (notebook section "Vector Dot Products").
  - His observation: it is a little hard to describe
    geometrically, but the black vector can grow pretty quickly,
    "as you would expect, multiplicatively," as the components
    get larger.
  - Example (added): c = (3, 4, 2) and g = (2, 1, 4) again.
    - c * g = (3 times 2, 4 times 1, 2 times 4) = (6, 4, 8).
    - Still a vector. Each entry is one line item's subtotal:
      $6 of bagels, $4 of coffee, $8 of muffins.
  - Reading the two side by side (added): elementwise
    multiplication is the itemized receipt. The dot product is
    the total at the bottom. Same numbers, one keeps the lines,
    the other adds them up.

6. DOT PRODUCT AS THE SUM OF THE ELEMENTWISE PRODUCT
  - The relationship he draws: the dot product is just the sum
    of the elementwise products. Notationally, he writes it with
    vertical bars around c * g to mean "the sum of that vector."
    c . g = |c * g|.
  - The chain: elementwise multiplication gives a vector of the
    same length as c and g. Add up its entries. That sum is the
    dot product. "You can see why it's equivalent."
  - The second demo adds the norms of a and b and the dot
    product a . b to the legend. The dot product is computed
    exactly this way, elementwise product then sum. See the
    visualizeDotProduct widget in the same notebook section.
  - His warning from the demo: it is difficult to see the
    relationship between the lengths of a and b and the dot
    product. It is not simply the norms multiplied together.
    Something else is going on.
  - Code (added): both operations on the coffee cart vectors.
      import numpy as np
      c,g = np.array([3,4,2]),np.array([2,1,4]) ## costs, order
      print(c*g) ## [6 4 8], elementwise
      print(np.sum(c*g), np.dot(c,g)) ## 18 18, dot both ways

7. THE ANGLE FORMULA
  - The second way to write the dot product: a . b equals the
    norm of a times the norm of b times the cosine of theta,
    where theta is the angle between a and b. He says the
    derivation comes from trigonometry and right triangles, and
    he is not going to spend time on it.
  - KEY TERM: theta: the angle between the two vectors, measured
    at the origin where both arrows start (added definition).
  - His description of cosine: it tells you the size of the
    angle horizontally across from the 90 degrees in a right
    triangle. Visually, imagine a right triangle wedged between
    a and b, with the angle between them at the corner.
  - His interpretation, the sentence to memorize: the dot
    product is the length of the first vector times the length
    of the second vector, "penalized by how aligned or not
    aligned the two vectors are." The cosine is the alignment
    share that weights the product of the lengths.
  - What cosine does as a number (added): it runs from 1 (angle
    zero, same direction) down through 0 (angle 90, no shared
    direction) to -1 (angle 180, opposite directions). So the
    dot product is the full product of the lengths when the
    vectors agree, nothing when they are perpendicular, and
    negative when they point against each other.
  - The demo changes knobs: instead of x and y components, you
    set each vector's angle and norm. The plot reports the norms,
    theta, the cosine, and the dot product. See the
    visualizeDotProduct2 widget in the same notebook section.
  - Example (added): a = (3, 0) and b = (2, 2).
    - Component way: 3 times 2 + 0 times 2 = 6.
    - Angle way: ||a|| = 3. ||b|| = square root of 8, about
      2.828. The angle between them is 45 degrees (a lies on the
      x axis, b sits halfway between the axes), and cos 45 is
      about 0.707. Multiply: 3 times 2.828 times 0.707 = 6.0.
    - Same answer by two roads. The component way is what you
      compute. The angle way is what it means.

8. THE TWO EXTREME CASES
  - No alignment: set the angle between a and b to 90 degrees.
    They share no directionality, so the cosine is 0, so the dot
    product is 0 no matter how long or short the vectors are.
    He makes them big and small on the slider to show the dot
    product stays at zero.
  - Perfect alignment: give both vectors the same angle. The
    cosine is 1 and the dot product is the product of the norms.
    His demo values: length of a set to 1, length of b is 2.4,
    dot product is 1 times 2.4 times 1, which is 2.4.
  - KEY TERM: perpendicular: at 90 degrees to each other, also
    called orthogonal. Two perpendicular vectors always have a
    dot product of zero (added definition). This fact comes back
    hard in Lecture 8.
  - Example (added): a = (3, 0) and b = (0, 5). One lies flat
    along x, the other stands straight up along y. Dot product:
    3 times 0 + 0 times 5 = 0. Lengths 3 and 5, alignment none,
    result zero.
  - Example (added): a = (1, 2) and b = (3, 6). b is a scaled
    up by 3, so they point the same way. Dot product: 1 times 3
    + 2 times 6 = 15. Norms: square root of 5 (about 2.236) and
    square root of 45 (about 6.708). Product of norms: 15.0.
    Cosine 1, full credit.
  - Example (added): a = (2, 1) and b = (-1, 2). Dot product:
    -2 + 2 = 0. Perpendicular, even though neither one lies on
    an axis. The dot product finds the right angle without a
    protractor.
  - Practical reading (added): the dot product is a similarity
    score. Large and positive means the two vectors mostly
    agree. Zero means unrelated directions. Negative means they
    oppose. That reading is exactly how it gets used later in
    data work, when the vectors are rows of features instead of
    grocery prices.

9. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Dot product: multiply matching components, add them up. Two
    vectors in, one number out. Written c . g.
  - The grocery budget is 30 = c . g. Costs dotted with counts.
  - The dot symbol means vector dot product between vectors and
    plain multiplication between components. Watch the context.
  - Elementwise multiplication (asterisk) multiplies matching
    components and keeps the vector. Sum that vector and you
    have the dot product.
  - Angle form: a . b = ||a|| ||b|| cos(theta). Product of the
    lengths, weighted by alignment.
  - 90 degrees apart: dot product zero, regardless of length.
    Same angle: dot product equals the product of the norms.

10. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - g is the amounts vector (apples, oranges, bread), c is the
    cost vector (2, 3, 5). Both length 3.
  - The vector dot product is the sum of the componentwise
    products, written with a dot: c . g = sum of c_i times g_i.
  - The budget equation in vector form: 30 = c . g.
  - The subscript i denotes a component (a number), the arrow
    on top denotes a vector.
  - The dot has two meanings: vector dot product between vectors,
    ordinary multiplication between components. The professor
    calls this confusing and unfortunate.
  - Elementwise multiplication is written with an asterisk and
    returns a vector of the same length.
  - The dot product equals the sum of the elementwise product,
    written with vertical bars: c . g = |c * g|.
  - Angle formula: a . b = ||a|| times ||b|| times cos(theta),
    theta the angle between a and b. It comes from trigonometry
    and right triangles.
  - Interpretation: the product of the two lengths penalized by
    how aligned the vectors are.
  - Angle 90 degrees: cosine 0, dot product 0 regardless of the
    lengths. Same angle: cosine 1, dot product equals the product
    of the norms. Demo values: lengths 1 and 2.4, dot product
    2.4.
